# Develop and select a visual concept

Use for open creative development before committing to production. Preserve approved concepts for execution-only work. Develop each brief on its own terms. Read the optional case study (external resource; not included) when a worked example helps; do not default to its theme, style, reveal, or tools.

## Generate ideas with a reason to exist

If you have an interesting observation or feeling but no complete story, try a short rough edit or scene first. Read the examples from The Curse (external resource; not included), then use the selection step below before full production.

Start with a specific human observation, desire, question, or tension and the actual source/product characteristics available. Find a relationship that can become visible: a familiar feeling made physical, a product detail expanded into a world, an ordinary behavior exaggerated, a hidden mechanism revealed, or a change of scale or viewpoint that changes our understanding. These are starting moves, not a required formula or a reason to force surrealism into every brief.

Develop a small set of meaningfully different premises, usually three when breadth is unspecified. Changing palette, lens, model, wardrobe, or setting alone does not make a new premise. For each, write a compact account of:

- The human experience and why this audience might care.
- The central visible action or relationship, in one plain sentence.
- The product/source connection, where relevant.
- What the viewer sees first and the concrete payoff or final image. A single still may contain both.
- The hardest thing the execution must make legible.

Do not substitute titles, mood adjectives, or “then something surprising happens” for a developed idea. For a reveal, work backward from the ending: what earlier details must be present so it feels earned? The ending should change or complete the meaning of what came before. It need not be a twist when another payoff serves the brief better.

## Choose before multiplying executions

Compare the premises for recognizable human meaning, visual legibility, specificity, product relevance when applicable, payoff, and feasibility within the available resources. Explain why the recommended idea is stronger and name its main risk. Use judgment rather than invented numerical precision; internal preference is not audience validation.

Pressure-test the leading idea:

- Can the actual action or image communicate the idea without a paragraph explaining it?
- Is there a concrete payoff beyond “looks beautiful” or “uses AI”?
- For product creative, would an unrelated product fit equally well? If so, examine whether the connection is too generic.
- Does the ending follow from the setup, or is it an unrelated product shot appended afterward?
- Is the choice driven by meaning, or merely by the easiest thing the generator produces?

Make a cheap representative execution—a rough storyboard, keyframe pair, or simple visual study—to expose the biggest uncertainty. For a reveal-led film, inspect setup and ending together, including how the transition can preserve the relevant motif, scale, or spatial relationship. Do not require expensive animation to discover that the ending has not been designed.

Once the premise is credible, refine its expression through the sustained image-and-edit iteration described in AI creative judgment (external resource; not included). Ten to fifty variations of a weak premise do not establish concept quality. Return to the premise if trials reveal a conceptual problem; otherwise preserve it while improving execution. Present these concrete options and the representative execution at the human direction checkpoint (external resource; not included). Recommend a choice, then wait for selection before full production unless the user explicitly delegated that decision. Preparing alternatives does not authorize choosing on the user's behalf.
