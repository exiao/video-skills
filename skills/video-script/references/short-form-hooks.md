# Short-form hooks

Use this guide when a script needs a TikTok, Reels, Shorts, or other short-form opening. The first seconds have two related jobs: the image or action earns a pause, and the spoken or on-screen language earns the next few seconds. Write both together.

## Opening fields

Record these fields in the first scene or hook note:

- **First frame/action:** what is visible or changing before the viewer has read anything; it should be legible on a small, muted screen.
- **Spoken hook:** when needed, a brief, speakable line that names the audience's situation, creates a question, or makes a specific promise. A legible visual situation can establish the hook before speech begins.
- **On-screen line:** optional text that complements the voice instead of merely repeating it. Keep it short enough to read in the opening beat, or give a deliberately long text overlay sufficient hold time and legible sizing.
- **Open loop and payoff:** what the viewer is invited to wonder, and the concrete answer, proof, or result the script will actually deliver.
- **Next beat:** the immediate confirmation that makes staying feel justified.

## Useful patterns

Choose the pattern that fits the audience and the truthful story; do not force every video into a social hook.

- **Result first:** show the changed state or evidence, then ask how it happened.
- **Specific test:** name an action and a bounded condition, then promise the result of the test.
- **Problem-first call-out:** name a recognizable frustration or decision before introducing a product or solution.
- **Audience or POV:** let a defined viewer recognize their situation immediately.
- **Specificity stack:** build one second-person moment from several plausible pressures, ending on a small detail that reveals the feeling. For a silent text-overlay clip, the complete paragraph may be the hook; give it enough screen time to read instead of forcing it into a brief spoken line. Distinguish a clearly framed fictional POV from observed customer evidence. See short-form packaging (external resource; not included) for the writing pattern.
- **Sourced practitioner tip:** name a specific stuck point and an unusual tip from a real, accurately attributed source, then deliver the first useful step in the next beat. Do not imply a private consultation or proven outcome from a generic template. See short-form packaging (external resource; not included).
- **Search-task title:** for a guide or answer viewers might look up, put their natural query on the first slide and start delivering the answer immediately. The title need not manufacture suspense; keep the visuals and following beats aligned with the named task. See short-form packaging (external resource; not included).
- **Contradiction or comparison:** put two plausible choices, outcomes, or beliefs in tension and resolve the difference later.

For a cold audience, test a viewer/problem-first opening before a product-first opening when the product is not itself the story. A high-intent demo may need the product in frame immediately. The promise must remain accurate either way.

For a crowded niche, translate the category or method into a concrete, surprising situation before explaining the mechanism. Read short-form packaging (external resource; not included) for premise choices, faceless slideshow examples, and claim checks. Build intrigue from supported details rather than inflating the result. For silent slideshows, the first-slide text and image carry the opening; omit the spoken-hook field and use slide two as the next beat. Hook-copy-only requests belong to hooks; full slide sequences can use this script workflow.

## Witness, assumption, and reversal

Use this optional pattern when the reveal changes how viewers understand the opening. Record what the viewer witnesses, what they are likely to assume, what challenges that assumption, and what the reveal explains. The change should matter to the story or product. Preserve enough continuity that viewers can connect the two interpretations; a surprise with no explanatory connection is not a payoff.

Shared reference example: [thebranding.ai's reel](https://www.instagram.com/reel/Dbav99SMNwA/) opens on an apparently candid recording of a man eating alone, with an overlay judging the situation. The framing then becomes more composed and the man addresses the camera. The breakdown presents this as a planned performance, changing the viewer's role from observer of an unaware subject to audience of someone directing the scene. The transferable sequence is an observed situation, an initial interpretation, a detail that challenges it, and a reveal that explains the setup. Do not copy the example's ridicule or pass a staged scene off as real customer evidence.

The reel's caption advertises three triggers; its narration names a witness hook and a pattern break, then describes the reveal. Its claim of 14 million views, attribution of sharing to these techniques, and assertions about automatic brain responses are creator claims, not verified causal evidence. Treat this pattern as a creative option to test, not a psychological guarantee. Direct demos and useful answers need no reversal when their value is already clear.

## Script checks

For story-led ads, make the opening pose a situation-level question: what does this character want, what blocks them, and what will they try? Start at the meaningful action or disruption rather than a generic product introduction. The next beat should change the situation, not pause for a benefits monologue. Use story-led ad guidance (external resource; not included) to connect the product's real mechanism to the eventual payoff. This is one format option, not a mandatory character arc for every demo or compact joke.

Read the line aloud. Remove throat-clearing, generic greetings, and explanations that close the question before the next beat. Give each spoken beat one job and a visual reason to continue. If the opening promises a reveal, comparison, or result, identify where it is paid off rather than manufacturing suspense.

When an opening underperforms, hold the body of the piece constant and test a different first frame, spoken line, or pairing before rewriting the whole format. Use comparable baselines and actual retention or downstream outcomes; do not treat a universal view or retention threshold as proof.

After a topic change or natural attention dip, add one relevant rehook: a new question, consequence, proof point, or visual change. It should advance the story rather than add random motion or filler.

## Title and subtitle roles

When the script includes both a title hook and speech captions, label them separately in the existing scene note; their text and placement have different jobs. Keep the title short enough to fit clear space above the subject without forcing it against the top edge. For a spatial lesson, pair the words with an actual visible demonstration or comparison.
