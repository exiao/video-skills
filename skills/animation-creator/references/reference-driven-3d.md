# Reference-driven 3D recreation

Use this when the user asks for a code-authored 3D restaging of an existing animation. This is an execution path within Animation Creator. Use [reference-style-video](../../reference-style-video/SKILL.md) to decide what the user wants preserved or replaced and to analyze the source. Keep the selected renderer or native format if the user specifies one; Blender is an option, not a requirement.

## Measure the actual clip

Retrieve the named attachment or use its authorized local copy. A post can include multiple media items or a quoted video; identify the exact clip before measuring. Probe dimensions, frame rate, frame count, duration, cuts, and audio. Decide whether the output should be silent, retain a supplied track, or use new audio, and carry that decision through encoding and verification. Extract a contact sheet to navigate, then inspect the whole motion where possible. Build a compact beat sheet for actions that make the recreation recognizable: start and end poses, prop handoffs, contact and release, path changes, expression changes, holds, camera motion, and material or shape transitions. Record frame or time anchors and mark observations separately from inferred 3D geometry. Measure only as finely as the requested fidelity warrants; dense frame tracking is useful for very short motion with fast interactions, but is not a default requirement.

For a single continuous scene, do not invent cuts. Measure stable landmarks such as character bounds, prop endpoints, ground-contact points, horizon or backdrop edge, and shadow direction at representative frames. Where the camera moves, distinguish camera motion from subject motion using more than one independent background and foreground cue when available; a change in subject size alone does not prove zoom. Keep a source-frame index convention explicit so one-based exported image names do not shift an otherwise aligned comparison. For stepped or handmade-looking motion, identify holds and duplicated frames as part of the performance rather than smoothing them away.

## Build the scene around contact and state

Confirm the selected 3D renderer and encoder are available, note the renderer version, and check that the output directory is writable. Choose geometry, materials, lighting, and camera to match the requested look. Test silhouette, scale, framing, and set lighting in a few representative stills before full animation. A matched palette alone does not recreate tactile appearance: compare edge softness, surface variation, shadow width and direction, depth of field, and any deliberate held frames.

Represent actions with frame-indexed, seekable poses or native keyframes. Keep measured tracks separate from scene code when measurements are dense, and keep the camera, actors, props, and material state independently adjustable. For an interacting prop, track both contact point and holder/grip. Derive the prop pose from those anchors when possible; its visible motion must agree with the contact surface and the trace it creates. For a drawn or assembled object, separate the stages: mark or outline on the surface, release or peel, change of orientation, gain in thickness, fill or material change, and final pose. The stages may happen quickly, but rendering the final object too early collapses the payoff.

If reference measurements are in screen pixels, use calibrated camera projection or ray/plane intersections to map them into scene coordinates. Check depth assumptions at contacts rather than assuming every measured point lies on one plane. Avoid forcing an approximate pixel track through a physical action when it causes sliding, floating, or a line to run ahead of its tool.

## Test the hardest beats before full export

Render a small set of matched source/output frames or a short motion sample covering the most failure-prone action: a throw and catch, floor contact, shape change, camera move, or occlusion. Compare at the same frame or timestamp. For dense tracking, run the same landmark or color detector on source and output so offsets are measured consistently. Check the tool-to-change registration separately from character position: a pencil, blade, or foot must meet the mark or surface it affects. Fix geometry and camera first, then action anchors, then material and lighting. When a contact path is wrong, measure its error at the specific frame and revise the relevant motion keys rather than globally shifting the scene. Preserve improvements outside the failing range; give Claude one or two frame-specific gaps and a keep-list, then test those frames before another full render.

For fast jumps or camera follows, inspect flight peaks between the chosen stills. When useful, project evaluated render-visible subject vertices through the camera at every flight frame; exclude unrelated cards, controls and props, and leave room for blur/DOF beyond geometric bounds. A bounded camera repair should preserve accepted states outside its window. Rerender neighboring frames affected by shutter sampling or curve interpolation too, and keep one accepted source version for a long render. The [Clawd recipe](clawd-motion-studio.md#inspect-flight-peaks-between-preview-stills) records three actual between-still crops and their repairs.

Render the complete sequence only after the sample works. Keep editable scene code or a native project, a reproducible render command, and an encoded preview. Verify the final frame count, dimensions, duration, streams, and full decode. Inspect matching frames from the **encoded** output and watch playback when available; frame pairs alone cannot establish smoothness or the feel of a transition. Report matches and remaining differences in action, timing, geometry, and appearance separately. Do not claim an exact recreation merely because duration and keyframe count match.

## Worked example and its limits

In the pencil-friend recreation, Claude wrote a procedural Blender scene from a measured short clip, rendered selected frames, revised the pencil contact and paper-to-cube transition, then encoded and compared an 86-frame silent video. Its character proportions, palette, frame keys, Blender version, and render settings belong to that source clip. The remaining clean-edged paper look shows why a technically aligned sequence still needs material and motion critique. Do not copy its scene or numbers as a universal template.
